Amammerɛ, Mfomso, ne Akwankyerɛ

Chapter 4 · Culture Notes, Common Mistakes & Tips for Success

Kɔ so sua!
A

Cultural Notes

🕐 Ghanaian Time

  • Social events almost always start later than announced. A party at 4 PM may not begin until 6 PM. Do not be offended — this is normal.
  • "Mereba" (I am coming) can mean "I am on my way right now" or "I will come at some point." Context tells you which.
  • For business meetings, hospital appointments, and tro-tro departures — be on time. The flexibility applies to social life, not professional settings.
  • "Ɛnnɛ ara" (today itself) often means "sometime today," not "immediately."

👨‍👩‍👧 Family Obligations

  • In Ghana, "abusua" (family) is not just parents and siblings — it includes grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, and sometimes the whole village.
  • If you have money, you are expected to help family members. This is not a burden — it is an honour and a social norm.
  • Children often live with extended family, not just their parents. It takes a village to raise a child — literally.
  • Never refuse food when visiting a family home. Even saying "Medaase, madi" (Thank you, I have eaten) is more polite than a flat refusal.

📛 Day Names and Identity

  • Akan day names (Kradin) are not just names — they carry spiritual meaning. Each day is associated with a spirit and a set of character traits.
  • When you meet someone named Kofi, Ama, or Akosua, you already know which day they were born without asking.
  • It is perfectly normal to ask "Wo kradin no yɛ da bɛn?" (Which day does your name come from?) — people love talking about this.
  • Many Ghanaians use both their English name and their Akan day name interchangeably.

🛒 Market Etiquette

  • Always greet before you buy. Walking straight to a stall and asking the price without a greeting is considered rude.
  • Bargaining is expected and normal. "Te boɔ ase kakra" (Reduce the price a little) is not an insult — it is part of the conversation.
  • Market sellers are often happy to hear you try Twi. Even a few words will earn you goodwill and sometimes a better price.
  • Use your right hand to give and receive money or goods.

🎂 Birthdays and Age

  • Birthdays are increasingly celebrated in Ghana, especially in cities, but the Western concept of a birthday party is relatively recent.
  • Some older Ghanaians do not know their exact birth date and may find it awkward to be asked directly. Be sensitive.
  • Asking age is generally acceptable in Ghana — it is less taboo than in some Western cultures, especially between people of similar age.
  • Respect for elders is central: always greet older people first, use "agya" (sir) and "ɛna" (ma) even for non-relatives.

🗣️ Asking for Directions

  • Always start with a greeting before asking for directions — "Maakye! Mepa wo kyɛw..."
  • Directions in Ghana are often landmark-based, not street-name based: "Pass the church, turn right at the big mango tree."
  • If you are lost, people are generally very willing to help. Do not hesitate to ask multiple people if needed.
  • "Ɛbɛn" (close/nearby) can be subjective — what a local considers "close" may be a 20-minute walk.
B

Common Mistakes to Avoid

❌ Mistake: Saying "Me di mfeɛ aduonu" for "I am 20 years old." ✅ Fix: Use "Mewɔ mfeɛ aduonu." — "wɔ" (have) is the correct verb for age, not "di" (eat).
❌ Mistake: Putting the adjective before the noun — "kɛseɛ onipa" (big person). ✅ Fix: Adjective always comes AFTER the noun in Twi: "onipa kɛseɛ."
❌ Mistake: Confusing "ahe?" and "sɛn?" — using them interchangeably for all quantity questions. ✅ Fix: "Ahe?" is for countable things (mma ahe? — how many children?). "Sɛn?" is for uncountable or general amounts (sika sɛn? — how much money?).
❌ Mistake: Forgetting to greet before asking a question or buying at the market. ✅ Fix: Always start with "Maakye / Maaha / Maadwo" + "Mepa wo kyɛw..." before your question. In Ghana, the greeting is not optional — it is part of respect.
❌ Mistake: Using "nna" and "nnora" interchangeably for "yesterday." ✅ Fix: "Nnora" is the standard word for yesterday. "Ndɔ" is also used. "Nna" alone does not mean yesterday — be careful with tone.
❌ Mistake: Translating "I am going to the market" as "Meyɛ kɔ ɛdwam". ✅ Fix: Simply say "Mekɔ ɛdwam." — Twi does not need "am going to" as a separate construction. The verb "kɔ" already carries the meaning.
C

Tips for Success

🔢

Drill your numbers daily

Numbers are the hardest part of this chapter to retain. Say the tens (du, aduonu, aduasa...) every morning until they are automatic. Then practice compound numbers with a friend — one person calls an English number, the other says the Twi.

📅

Use the days every day

Every morning when you wake up, say the day in Twi before you say it in English. "Ɛnnɛ yɛ Dwoada." Within a week it will be automatic.

👨‍👩‍👧

Describe your real family

Write out your actual family in Twi — real names, real ages, real locations. Personalised sentences stick far better than generic textbook examples. Carry the list with you and review it.

🕐

Practice time with your phone

Every time you check your phone for the time, say it in Twi first. "Abɔ nnɔnwɔtwe ne fa." This passive practice adds up to hundreds of repetitions a week with zero extra study time.

🏷️

Find out your Akan day name

If you do not know which day of the week you were born, find out — ask your parents, check your birth certificate, or look it up online. Then introduce yourself with your Akan name: "Me din de [name]. Me kradin de [day name]."

🛒

Use Twi at a Ghanaian market or shop

Go to a Ghanaian shop, market, or restaurant and try buying something entirely in Twi. Greet, ask the price, bargain, count the money. Even if you make mistakes, you will be welcomed for trying.

📝

Write one sentence about today — every day

Combine everything from this chapter in one daily sentence: "Ɛnnɛ yɛ Efiada. Abɔ nnɔnwɔtwe. Mekɔ ɛdwam." (Today is Friday. It is 8 o'clock. I am going to the market.) Short, real, and useful.
